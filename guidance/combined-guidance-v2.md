# Combined emergency guidance

Version 2, 23/11/2026

A combined summary of household emergency guidance from the UK (UK Government, British Red Cross, Ready Scotland), Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Finland, Germany and France, with UK fire service, UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) and NHS advice added on fire, chemical and nuclear incidents, and energy needs. Where the guides disagreed, one approach has been chosen and is given here.

Everything is planned for **7 days**.

---

## Getting ready (applies to every scenario)

### Know your risks
- Find out what is likely where you live: flooding, storms, long power cuts, extreme weather, wildfire, and any nearby industrial or nuclear sites.
- Being prepared lets help go first to those who need it most.

### Staying informed
- Keep an **FM radio that works without mains power (battery or wind-up)**, with **spare batteries**. A car radio is a fallback.
- Write down the frequencies of the national and local stations that carry emergency news.
- Emergency alerts may be sent to mobile phones in the area. Keep phones charged and alerts switched on.
- Follow official sources and trusted broadcasters. Be wary of anything that provokes a strong reaction, check it against official sources, and don't pass on rumours. Criminals use emergencies for scams.

### Contacts
- Keep a **paper list** of important numbers (family, neighbours, GP, pharmacy, vet, insurer, utilities), because phones may be flat or networks down. Keep it somewhere easy to find in the dark.
- Add emergency contacts and medical information to your phone (ICE or Medical ID).
- When networks are busy, only call if you need help.

### Cash and payments
- Card payments and cash machines may not work in a power cut or cyber attack.
- Keep **£100 per person in cash, in mixed notes and coins**, plus more than one bank card with known PINs.
- Use the cash now and then so it isn't forgotten, and top it back up.

### Documents
- Keep **copies** of key documents in a **waterproof pouch**, ready to take: ID and passports, birth certificates, insurance details, prescriptions and the medication list, medical equipment instructions, and the dogs' vet details, vaccination records and microchip numbers.
- Keep **backup copies in cloud storage** as well.
- Keep spare house and car keys.

### Medicines and first aid
- Keep **as much stock of regular medicines as you can**, with a **written list** of medicines and doses. Order repeat prescriptions as early as you're allowed, so you never run low.
- Keep a stock of non-prescription medicines (painkillers and so on).
- First aid kit: waterproof plasters, bandages, sterile dressings, medical tape, antiseptic, eyewash, a thermometer, tweezers, gloves and painkillers. Add a dog first aid kit (vet wrap, tick remover, paw balm).
- Check expiry dates and throw out old medicines.
- Learn basic first aid, including how to stop severe bleeding.
- Keep spare glasses.

### Neighbours and community
- Swap contact details with neighbours. Think about a street messaging group.
- Agree who checks on whom, especially anyone living alone or with health needs.
- Check on others once your own household is safe.
- Anyone using powered medical equipment, or with a disability or long-term health condition, can join their electricity and water companies' **Priority Services Register**.

### Your home
- Know how to turn off the **gas, electricity and water**. If the gas has been turned off, don't turn it back on yourself.
- Fit **one smoke or heat alarm on each floor, in the hall and on the landing**. Fire services also suggest a heat alarm in the kitchen. Ten-year sealed alarms, linked so that all of them sound together, are best.
- Fit a **carbon monoxide alarm** in any room where a gas heater, stove or fuel-burning appliance is used.
- Keep a **fire blanket** in the kitchen.
- Check your insurance covers your risks, including flooding, and keep the policy details with your documents.
- Photograph your home and contents; it helps with insurance claims.

## Scenario A: Staying at home
*Storm, long power cut, loss of water or gas, or being told to stay indoors*

### Water
- Store **3 litres per person per day** for drinking and cooking: **21 litres per person for 7 days**. Add water for the dogs: about 40–60 ml per kg of body weight a day, more in warm weather or after exercise.
- Use bottled water or clean food-grade containers with lids. Keep it cool and dark.
- **Check stored water twice a year** for taste, smell and dates, and replace it if needed.
- More water is needed for washing and flushing. If a cut is expected, **fill the bath, buckets and containers**.
- If you're unsure whether water is safe, bring it to a rolling boil. Rain and river water may be contaminated.
- Water in the hot water tank can be used for washing.

### Food
- **7 days** of long-life food that you normally eat, stored at room temperature. Most of it should be edible cold, so you're not dependent on cooking.
- Choose filling, energy-rich food: tins (meat, fish, vegetables, beans, soup), crispbread, pasta, rice, oats, dried fruit, nuts, peanut butter, cooking oil, long-life milk and fruit juice.
- **Energy needs.** Adults in their 50s need, on average:
  - **about 2,500–2,600 kcal a day for a man**;
  - **about 2,000–2,100 for a woman**.

  These are averages; size and activity change them. In a cold, unheated home, allow **an extra 100–200 kcal a day** (an extra snack), plus hot food and drinks. Warm layers matter more than extra food.
- As a guide, per person for 7 days (based on about 2,200 kcal a day; a man needs about a sixth more):

  | Food | Amount |
  |---|---|
  | Bread, cereals, potatoes, pasta, rice | 2.45 kg |
  | Vegetables and pulses | 2.8 kg |
  | Fruit and nuts | 1.75 kg |
  | Milk and milk products | 1.8 kg |
  | Fish, meat and eggs | 1.05 kg |
  | Fats and oils | 0.25 kg |

- Build the store up gradually, adding one or two items each time you shop. Rotate it by using and replacing.
- In a power cut, eat chilled food first, then frozen food. Don't refreeze food that has thawed.
- Don't forget a **tin opener**.
- Keep **7 days of dog food**.

### Cooking without power
- Keep plenty of no-cook food, **plus a way to cook without electricity**: a gas hob that can be lit by hand, or a camping stove with spare cartridges. Keep matches or a lighter.
- Camping stoves are designed for outdoor use. Use them in a well-ventilated place, with a carbon monoxide alarm, and never leave them unattended.
- Change gas cartridges outdoors, away from flames.
- **Never use a barbecue, charcoal grill or generator indoors**: they give off carbon monoxide.

### Keeping warm
- Homes get cold quickly when the heating stops.
- Have **warm clothing** (wool layers, thick socks, hats, gloves, scarves), **blankets, duvets, sleeping bags and sleeping mats**. These are core items.
- **Gather in one warm room.** Close its doors, hang blankets over the windows, and put rugs on the floor.
- **Using a portable gas heater.** Fire services advise using only heaters designed for indoor use. If a portable camping-type gas heater is the only heat available in a long, freezing outage, keep the risk as low as possible:
  - use it in **short bursts** to top up the warm room, not continuously;
  - stand it on a **fireproof mat**, at least **1 metre** from furniture, curtains, people and dogs;
  - keep a **window partly open** and a **carbon monoxide alarm** in the room;
  - **never leave it unattended**, never move it while it's lit, and **switch it off before sleeping**;
  - check the seal and fitting each time, and don't use a damaged unit;
  - change cartridges outdoors.
- **Gas cartridges in the cold.** Butane works poorly below freezing. Keep spare cartridges cool and dry, below 50 °C, but above 0 °C; in freezing weather, store them in the warm room, away from the heater.
- **Carbon monoxide** has no smell. Headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion and tiredness are warning signs. If you suspect it, or the alarm sounds: switch the appliance off, get into fresh air, and call 999 if anyone feels unwell.
- If the home becomes too cold, go and stay with family or friends.

### Light and power
- **Torches or head torches**, with plenty of spare batteries; wind-up torches are useful. Torches are safer than candles and should be the first choice.
- **Candles**, with matches, as well:
  - in a proper holder on a stable, non-flammable surface;
  - away from curtains, furniture and anything that can burn, and out of reach of the dogs;
  - never left unattended, and put out before sleeping or leaving the room;
  - don't carry lit candles between rooms.
- **Charged power banks** for phones and lights, plus an in-car phone charger. Use phones sparingly to save battery.
- When the power goes, switch off equipment (especially the hob and heaters) so nothing comes back on unattended, but leave one light on to show when the power is back. Report power cuts on **105**.

### Communications in a power cut
- Mobile networks and digital landlines may fail. Wi-Fi calling can help while broadband is still working.
- Keep the paper contact list to hand.

### Hygiene and toilets without water
- Hand sanitiser, wet wipes, toilet paper, soap, toothpaste, sanitary products, disposable gloves, kitchen roll and rubbish bags are core items. Disposable plates and cutlery save water.
- **If the toilet can't be flushed:**
  - put a strong bag (or two) in the toilet bowl, or use a bucket with a lid or a camping toilet;
  - cover each use with sawdust or cat litter to cut smell;
  - tie the bags and store them outside until you're told how to get rid of them.
- Keep food preparation clean and wash hands often.

### Dogs
- At least **7 days** of dog food and water, and medicines.
- Also keep bedding, waste bags, leads and harnesses, and the vet's number written down.
- Keep dogs calm and indoors in severe weather, and well away from heaters, stoves and candles.

### Storms and severe weather
- Storms bring power cuts, flooding, fallen trees and travel disruption: prepare for both power cuts and flooding.
- Before a storm, secure loose items outside.
- During a storm, stay indoors, away from windows. Don't travel unless you must. Keep at least 50 m away from fallen power lines.
- In snow and ice, rural homes may be cut off: have extra warm clothing, bedding and battery lighting.
- In a heatwave: extra drinking water, keep medicines cool, and use fans and cooling towels.

### Chemical or nuclear incident: being told to stay indoors
**Go in, stay in, tune in.** Leave only if you're told to. The exception is a chemical release close to you outdoors, when you should move away from it, upwind if you can.

**If you were outside when it happened:**
- Go into the nearest solid building, even if it isn't your home.
- Take off your outer clothing carefully, without pulling it over your head. Cut it off if you need to. This removes most of any contamination.
- Put the clothes and shoes in a bag and leave it outside or in a separate room.
- Don't eat, drink or smoke until you have washed.
- **Chemical:** blot or brush the substance off the skin, then rinse with plenty of water if the skin itches or hurts.
- **Radiation:** shower with soap and shampoo (no conditioner), then put on clean clothes that were stored indoors.
- Wash any pet that was outside (wear gloves) and keep it indoors.

**Sealing the house:**
- Close all doors and windows, including trickle vents.
- Switch off fans, extractor fans, air conditioning, and any heating or ventilation that draws in outside air.
- Bring pets in only if they're with you. Don't go out to look for one.

**Which room to use:**
- UK guidance says go to the **middle of the building, away from outside doors, windows and walls**.
- **If every room has windows:** choose the room with the **fewest and smallest windows**, on the side of the house away from the incident, and keep away from the glass.
- **Chemical:** an upstairs room is better, because many chemicals are heavier than air.
- **Radiation:** a central room on a lower floor is better.

**While sheltering:**
- Tune in to local radio, TV or official websites, and stay in until you're told it's safe. That's usually **1–2 days** for radiation, or a number of hours for a chemical release.
- Food and drink already indoors, covered food and **mains tap water are safe** unless you're told otherwise. Don't eat garden produce or drink water stored outside or from wells until advised.
- Don't go out to collect children from school; schools will look after them.
- Use phones only when needed, to keep lines free. Call **999** in a real emergency.
- If you need medicines, call **NHS 111** rather than going out.

**Iodine tablets**
- Stable iodine (potassium iodate) **protects only the thyroid gland, and only against radioactive iodine**. It does nothing against other radiation.
- **Take it only when officially told to, and only the tablets provided.** Never use iodised salt or other products.
- Tablets are issued in advance only to homes very close to a nuclear site. Further out, they're handed out in an emergency if needed.
- **Timing matters:**
  - one dose protects for 24 hours;
  - it works best taken up to 24 hours before, or within 2 hours after, exposure;
  - it still helps up to 8 hours after;
  - don't start it more than 24 hours after exposure.
- Adults and children over 12 usually take 2 tablets (85 mg tablets). **Follow the leaflet with the tablets you are given.**
- People with an iodine allergy, or certain rare skin and blood vessel conditions, should not take it.
- There is no official UK advice to buy your own. The plan is to follow official instructions.

### Coping
- Being prepared reduces stress.
- Anxiety and poor sleep are normal. Keep routines, eat and sleep well, stay in touch with others, and limit how much news you take in.
- Have books, games and puzzles that don't need power.

## Scenario B: Leaving with notice
*About 1–2 hours to leave by car, for example for flood or wildfire*

### Deciding to go
- Don't wait to be told if you can see the risk building. You may get official warnings through alerts, radio or TV, but they may be late or patchy. Leave in good time, because roads will be slow and may close.
- **Plan possible routes in advance**:
  - a main route and at least one alternative;
  - leaving in different directions;
  - avoiding roads and dips that flood;
  - marked on a paper map.

  Know where the routes cross rivers or low ground.
- Keep the car's fuel tank at least half full (or an electric car charged).
- Return only when it's safe.

### What to take
Keep a **basic grab bag packed**, then use an **additions checklist** on the day to add what can't be stored in the bag.

**Packed grab bag (per person)**

| Item | Why |
|---|---|
| Copies of documents in a waterproof pouch | Proof of identity and insurance; needed to claim, rebook or register |
| Cash (£100 per person) and bank cards | Shops and fuel stations may not take cards in a power cut |
| Paper list of phone numbers | Phones may be flat, lost or without signal |
| Spare house and car keys | Usual keys may be left behind in a rush |
| Torch or head torch, spare batteries | Light without using the phone battery; a head torch leaves hands free |
| FM radio (battery or wind-up) | News and instructions when phones and internet fail |
| Power bank and phone charger | Keeps the phone working for calls, alerts and maps |
| First aid kit and non-prescription medicines | Treat cuts, sprains and pain when help is far away |
| Warm and waterproof clothing, hat, gloves, spare socks | Staying warm and dry prevents cold injury; wet clothes lose heat fast |
| Survival bag | Keeps a person warm and dry if stranded; works better than a foil blanket |
| Whistle | Carries much further than shouting, with little effort, to attract rescuers |
| Toiletries, wet wipes, hand sanitiser | Basic hygiene when water and washing are limited |
| Paper OS map of the area and a compass | Navigation with no signal or battery; shows routes, footpaths and high ground |
| Pen and notebook | Recording information, leaving notes |
| Dog leads, spare collar with ID tag, waste bags, collapsible bowl | Dogs must be controlled and identifiable; a bowl for water on the move |

**Additions on the day**

| Item | Why |
|---|---|
| Regular medicines and the medication list | Prescriptions can't be replaced quickly away from home |
| Glasses or contact lenses | Can't be replaced quickly |
| Mobile phones (charged) | Calls, alerts, maps |
| Water bottles filled from the tap | Safe drinking water may be hard to find |
| Food for a few days, snacks and a tin opener | Energy and comfort; shops may be shut |
| Dog food, dog medicines, bedding, a dog first aid kit | The dogs' needs away from home |
| Sleeping bags or blankets | Rest centres and other people's homes may be short of bedding |
| Valuables | Kept together so they can be collected quickly |
| Laptop or tablet and chargers | Access to cloud document copies and contacts |

### Shutting up the house
- **Switch off the electricity, gas and water** before leaving.
- **For flooding**, move valuables, documents and electrical items upstairs, and secure anything that could float or leak.
- Close and lock windows and doors.
- Leave a contact phone number for rescuers or neighbours, and tell family you have left.
- Accept that food in the fridge and freezer will spoil. Photograph it for your insurance claim.

### Where to go and meeting up
- **Local meeting point:** an agreed place near home, easy to find, sheltered and accessible, in case you're separated or can't get home.
- **Longer-term stay:** decide in advance where you would stay for days or weeks, such as family or friends outside the risk area who will take the dogs.
- Agree how you will contact each other if networks fail.

### Dogs
- Leads, a harness, and a collar with an ID tag showing the owner's name and address (required by law in public). Keep microchip details up to date.
- Food and water for several days, medicines, bedding, waste bags and a collapsible bowl.
- Vaccination records, the vet's details and a photo of each dog, in a dry bag. Rest centres may ask for vaccination records and may not take pets, so plan where the dogs can stay.

### Flooding
- Check your flood risk and sign up for warnings. Keep the Floodline number to hand.
- **Before:** sandbags or boards, valuables upstairs, documents in waterproof bags, medicines easy to reach.
- **During:** don't stay in a cellar; switch off the power; **never walk or drive through flood water** (30 cm of moving water can float a car); keep out of underground garages.
- **After:** photograph the damage for insurance, dry rooms out, have gas and electrics checked before use, and don't eat food that has been in flood water.

### The car
Keep in the car:
- a first aid kit, torch, phone charger and radio;
- warm clothing and blankets;
- water and snacks;
- high-visibility clothing and jump leads;
- an ice scraper, de-icer and a snow shovel;
- a paper map.

### If you have to go on foot
*If you've left by car but the roads become impassable, and you need to walk out of the area with the dogs*

- **Before setting off:**
  - tell someone your route and when you expect to arrive;
  - plan a route on the **OS map** using footpaths and high ground, avoiding flood water;
  - lock the car and leave a note in it with your phone number.
- **Carry only what is vital.** A rough guide is no more than about **10% of your body weight**, or around 7–10 kg for an adult. Fitness matters more than the figure. Heavy loads slow you down and tire you.
- **Bags:**
  - use a **rucksack**, so both hands are free for the dogs' leads;
  - a hip belt takes the weight off your shoulders;
  - line it with a bin bag and keep important items in dry bags, because rucksacks are not waterproof;
  - leave behind bags that need carrying by hand.
- **The vital items:**
  - **OS Explorer map (1:25,000) and a compass**: navigation without signal or battery;
  - phone and power bank;
  - head torch and spare batteries;
  - water and food;
  - waterproofs, warm layers, hat and gloves;
  - survival bag;
  - whistle;
  - first aid kit and regular medicines;
  - cash, cards and the document pouch.
- **For the dogs:**
  - leads and harnesses, with ID tags on their collars;
  - water and a collapsible bowl;
  - food for at least a day;
  - medicines and waste bags;
  - a coat for short-coated or older dogs;
  - a light for each collar.

  Check their paws for cuts and ice, and don't let them drink flood water. A fit, fully grown dog can carry about a tenth of its own weight in a dog pack, but not young, older, long-backed or flat-faced breeds. Small dogs can be carried in a sling or dog carrier.
- **Food on the move.** Walking with a pack uses roughly 250–500 kcal an hour. Eat a snack about every hour and drink regularly.
- **If you need help:**
  - call **999 or 112** and ask for the police, then Mountain Rescue if needed;
  - if there's no signal, give **six long whistle blasts (or torch flashes) in a minute, pause for a minute, then repeat**;
  - three blasts is the reply.

## Scenario C: House fire
*Leaving instantly*

### Preventing fire
- **Cooking:** never leave cooking unattended. Avoid chip pans and use a thermostat-controlled fryer instead.
- **Electrics:**
  - one plug per socket;
  - don't overload extension leads or plug one lead into another;
  - watch for scorch marks, hot plugs or flickering lights.
- **Charging:** don't charge phones, laptops or batteries overnight, under pillows or on beds. Never charge e-bikes or e-scooters in a bedroom, a hallway or on your way out, or while asleep or out.
- **Heaters:** keep them at least 1 metre from furniture and curtains. Never use them to dry clothes. Switch them off before sleeping.
- **Candles:** follow the safety rules above. Consider battery LED candles.
- **Smoking:** never in bed; use a proper ashtray.
- Barbecues, camping stoves and generators are not for indoor use.
- Have chimneys and stoves checked.

### Bedtime check
Every night:
- the cooker and hob are off;
- appliances are unplugged where possible, with no washing machine or dryer running overnight;
- heaters are off and candles are out;
- exits and the stairs are clear;
- house and window keys are where everyone can find them;
- a phone is within reach of the bed.

Closing internal doors at night slows a fire's spread, if that suits the household.

### Alarms and equipment
- **One smoke or heat alarm on each floor, in the hall and on the landing**, ideally linked so that they all sound together, plus a heat alarm in the kitchen if possible.
- **Test them monthly**, vacuum them every six months, and replace them after ten years.
- **Fire blanket in the kitchen**, easy to reach and not above the cooker. Use it on a small pan fire, or wrap it round someone whose clothes are on fire.
- A fire extinguisher is optional, useful only for a very small fire or one blocking your way out. Water, foam or CO2 types suit the home. Have it serviced and replace it as the maker says.
- **Don't tackle a fire yourself** unless it is very small, everyone else is out and your way out is clear. If in doubt, get out.
- **Never put water on a pan fire.** Don't move the pan; turn off the heat only if it's safe.
- For an electrical fire, unplug the appliance or switch off at the fuse box. Never use water.

### Escape plan
- Plan the **normal way out and a second way out** from every room, especially bedrooms. Make sure everyone in the household knows them.
- Keep escape routes clear and keys near exits.
- Agree a **meeting place outside**, away from the house.
- **Practise the plan**, including at night.

### If fire breaks out
- **Shout to warn everyone, and get out.** Don't stop for belongings.
- **Crawl low under smoke**, where the air is clearer.
- **Before opening a door, feel it with the back of your hand.** If it's warm, don't open it, because the fire is on the other side.
- Close doors behind you to slow the fire.
- **Call 999 once you're out.** Give your address and say whether anyone, including pets, is still inside.
- **Never go back in for anything**, pets included. Tell firefighters where they are.
- If your clothes catch fire: **stop, drop and roll**.

### If you're trapped
- Get everyone into **one room with a window**, ideally with a phone.
- Close the door and put bedding or towels along the bottom to keep smoke out.
- **Call 999**, then open the window and shout **"Help, fire!"**
- **Escaping from a window is only for the ground or first floor:**
  - drop bedding or cushions below to land on;
  - lower yourself out feet first, hang by your hands, then drop;
  - **don't jump**;
  - if the window won't open, break the glass in a bottom corner and cover the jagged edges with a blanket.
- Above the first floor, wait at the window to be rescued.

## Keeping things up to date
- **Every month:**
  - charge the power banks and all rechargeable torches and lanterns;
  - test the torches and the radio;
  - test the smoke, heat and carbon monoxide alarms.
- **Twice a year (for example when the clocks change):** check everything else:
  - food, water, medicine and first aid dates;
  - spare batteries and gas cartridges;
  - the grab bag contents;
  - the cash;
  - document copies and cloud backups.

  Vacuum the alarms. Replace anything used or out of date.
- **The plan:** review it whenever circumstances change (moving home, new medicines, health or care changes, new pets) and go through it together. Keep a printed copy where everyone knows to find it.
- Build the store up gradually rather than all at once.
