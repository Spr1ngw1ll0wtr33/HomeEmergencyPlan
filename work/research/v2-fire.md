# v2 research: home fire safety (UK official guidance)

Accessed 23/11/2026 unless stated. Pages read in full with curl (WebFetch could not resolve these hosts in this session). "Search summary only" marks points seen only in search-engine summaries, not on the page itself.

## 1. Fire extinguisher, fire blanket, both or neither?

**Overall position:** neither is required. Both are "other equipment you could consider". Smoke alarms come first. Fire services do not want people fighting fires. A fire blanket in the kitchen is the most consistently suggested item. An extinguisher is optional, for very small fires only.

- GOV.UK / Home Office, *Fire safety in the home (accessible version)*, England, © 2022 – https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/make-your-home-safe-from-fire/fire-safety-in-the-home-accessible-version
  - "Fire blankets are used to put out a fire or wrap a person whose clothes are on fire. They are best kept in the kitchen."
  - "Fire extinguishers can be useful if the fire is very small or blocking your exit. If you choose to use an extinguisher, always read the instructions before use and don't put yourself in danger."
  - "Don't tackle fires yourself. Leave it to the professionals."
  - Pan fire: "Don't take any risks. Turn off the heat if it's safe to do so. Never throw water over it. Don't tackle the fire yourself."
- Fire Kills, *Escape* – https://firekills.campaign.gov.uk/escape/ : "don't tackle the fire yourself unless it's very small and you have a clear escape route".
- Hampshire & Isle of Wight FRS, *Fire Safety Equipment* – https://www.hantsfire.gov.uk/?p=27050
  - "There is no single type of fire extinguisher that can deal with all types of fire." Water: wood, paper, fabrics. CO2 or dry powder: electrical. Wet chemical: cooking oils and fats. Foam, CO2 or powder: flammable liquids.
  - "Which fire extinguisher do I need? … Home or office: Water, foam or CO₂. Kitchen: Wet chemical. Garage or workshop: Dry powder or CO₂."
  - "Never use a fire extinguisher on cooking oil or chip pan fires, as it can spread the flames. Instead, cover the pan with a fire blanket."
  - CO2 extinguishers only in well-ventilated spaces. Dry powder should not be used "in enclosed spaces as the powder can make it hard to breathe and limit visibility". Never hold the horn of a CO2 extinguisher (it gets extremely cold).
  - Lifespan: "5 years for most extinguishers, 10 years for CO₂ units"; check the label. Do not put old extinguishers in general waste (hazardous waste).
  - Fire blanket: "Keep it in the kitchen. More than half of the fires we attend start in the kitchen. Only use it on a small fire … Always look for the British Standard mark."
  - "Always call 999 first if there's any danger — use a fire extinguisher only if it's safe to do so."
- Fire Kills leaflet *Fire equipment for the home* (ODPM/FETA, 2003–04; old, but the only official placement advice found) – https://www.ddfire.gov.uk/sites/default/files/attachments/fireequipmentforthehome_0.pdf
  - "A fire blanket is most useful in the kitchen." "A fire extinguisher is best placed in the hall and taken where needed." "Blankets and extinguishers should be easily accessible in an emergency and never placed above a cooker or heater."
  - Fire blanket "make sure it complies with British Standard BS EN 1869".
  - "Before tackling any fire, make sure that everyone is outside and that your escape route is clear"; "only tackle small fires which are in their early stages"; "If you are at all unsure … don't take risks"; "if in doubt, get out, stay out and call 999."
- West Sussex FRS, *Kitchen safety* (updated 9 Sep 2025) – https://www.westsussex.gov.uk/fire-emergencies-and-crime/west-sussex-fire-and-rescue-service/home-fire-safety/kitchen-safety
  - Pan fire: "don't move it"; "turn off the heat if it's safe to do so, but never lean over a pan to reach the controls"; "place a fire blanket over the pan if you have one – don't use a fire extinguisher on a pan of oil as the force of the extinguisher can spread the fire"; leave it to cool completely.
  - Chip pan: "never use water … this will cause a fireball".
  - Electrical fire: "pull the plug out, or switch the power off at the fuse box"; "smother the fire with a fire blanket or use a dry powder or carbon dioxide extinguisher"; "never use water on an electrical fire".
- Devon & Somerset FRS: the page was blocked (403). Search summary only: a fire blanket should carry "BS EN 1869:1997 or BS 7944:1999" – https://www.dsfire.gov.uk/safety/home/fire-safety-equipment
- Sizes: no UK fire and rescue service home page gives extinguisher sizes. LFB's business guidance (GN08) mentions sizes such as 2 kg CO2 / 34B (search summary only) – https://www.london-fire.gov.uk/media/cnyexexn/hand-held-portable-firefighting-equipment-gn08.pdf. *Not household advice.*
- **Conflict worth noting:** Hampshire says "Home: water, foam or CO2". A Which?/retail search summary suggested dry powder for homes, but Hampshire warns against dry powder in enclosed spaces. For the guide, use the official line: optional, small fires only, and read the label for the fire classes it covers.

## 2. House fire guidance

### Prevention
- GOV.UK (above): around half of home fires are caused by cooking; two fires a day are started by candles; "Every six days someone dies from a fire caused by a cigarette"; "About three fires a day are started by heaters"; faulty electrics cause about 4,000 fires a year.
- **Kitchen** (GOV.UK; Fire Kills *Prevention* https://firekills.campaign.gov.uk/prevention/): never leave cooking unattended; if you leave the kitchen, take pans off the heat or turn the hob off; avoid cooking after alcohol or when tired; keep handles from sticking out; keep tea towels, cloths and packaging away from the hob; clean grease from the grill and oven; check the cooker is off before bed or going out. If oil smokes, it is too hot: turn off the heat. Use a thermostat-controlled deep-fat fryer, or oven chips instead of a chip pan.
- **Electrical** (GOV.UK; Fire Kills; LFB *Electrical items* https://www.london-fire.gov.uk/safety/the-home/electrical-items/): one plug per socket; high-powered appliances such as washing machines get their own socket. Extension leads are usually rated 13 A, so do not exceed that total, do not "daisy-chain" leads, and fully unwind cable drums. Warning signs: scorch marks, hot plugs or sockets, fuses that blow or breakers that trip for no reason, flickering lights, burning smells. Unplug appliances when not in use or at bed. Register appliances and check recalls. Have electrics inspected at least every 10 years (5 if renting). Empty tumble-dryer fluff. Leave hair straighteners to cool on a heatproof surface. Never cover lights or equipment.
- **Chargers** (LFB): don't buy cheap counterfeit chargers; "never leave phones or laptops plugged in to charge overnight". Fire Scotland: "Never charge your mobile phone while you are asleep, or charge it and leave under your pillow."
- **E-bikes and e-scooters** – NFCC *Charge Safe* – https://nfcc.org.uk/our-services/campaigns/charge-safe/ : "Never charge … in a bedroom, hallway or other place where it could block your escape"; "Never leave a battery charging unattended. Don't charge it while you are asleep or away from home"; use the manufacturer's charger; don't cover it; plug straight into a wall socket, not an extension lead; unplug when charged. Warning signs: heat, swelling, leaking, hissing or popping, unusual smell, smoke. Stop using it at once. Buy UKCA/CE-marked products from reputable sellers.
- **Smoking** (GOV.UK; Fire Kills): smoking is "the most common cause of fire deaths in the home". Never smoke in bed or when drowsy or drinking. Smoke outdoors. Use a proper ashtray that can't tip, never a wastepaper basket. "Put them out. Right out!" Unplug e-cigarettes when charged.
- **Candles** (GOV.UK; Fire Kills): use a proper holder on a level surface; keep away from curtains, furniture and draughts; never leave unattended; put out on leaving the room and at night; keep away from children and pets; consider LED candles; keep matches and lighters out of children's reach.
- **Heaters** (GOV.UK; LFB *Portable heaters* https://www.london-fire.gov.uk/safety/the-home/portable-heaters-gas-fires-and-open-fires/): keep clear of curtains, bedding and furniture; never dry clothes on them; secure them against a wall; "Always sit at least one metre away from a heater"; turn off and cool before moving; have old electric heaters tested or replaced; check recalls. Fit a CO alarm in rooms with solid-fuel, gas or paraffin heaters. Change gas cylinders in the open air. Use fireguards, and sweep chimneys yearly for coal, twice a year for logs.
- **Electric blankets** (GOV.UK): unplug before getting into bed unless it has an all-night thermostat; store flat, rolled or loosely folded; avoid second-hand ones.
- **Furniture** must carry the fire-resistant permanent label (GOV.UK).

### Bedtime check (GOV.UK, plus Fire Scotland *Bedtime checks* https://www.firescotland.gov.uk/at-home/bedtime-checks/)
- Close inside doors. Turn off and unplug appliances not designed to stay on (not the freezer). Check the cooker is off. Don't run washing machines, tumble dryers or dishwashers overnight. Turn heaters off and put up fireguards. Put out candles and cigarettes properly. Keep exits clear. Keep door and window keys where everyone can find them.
- Fire Scotland adds: pour water on cigarette ends before binning them; don't build up an open fire before bed; electric blanket off unless marked for all-night use; don't charge your phone while asleep; use laptops on hard surfaces and unplug them.
- Phone and torch within reach of the bed: Northumberland FRS, Devon & Somerset FRS; Fire Kills for older people (see gap-research.md §1).

### Smoke and heat alarms
- Fire Kills *Detection* – https://firekills.campaign.gov.uk/detect/ ; GOV.UK:
  - At least one on every level, on the ceiling, in the middle, in the hall and on the landing. "For extra safety, fit smoke alarms in the bedrooms too", and in the most-used rooms. Heat alarm in the kitchen. No smoke alarms in or near kitchens or bathrooms.
  - Ten-year sealed-battery alarms are "the best option for most people". Look for the Kitemark or LPCB mark. Mains alarms need an electrician.
  - Interlinked alarms all sound together; "useful if you live in a large house or over several levels".
  - "Test your smoke alarms at least monthly". Press the button until it sounds. Vacuum them every six months. A regular chirp means replace the battery now. Never remove the battery after a false alarm. Replace alarms after ten years or by the expiry date.
  - You are "around 11 times more likely to die in a fire if you do not have any working smoke alarms" (Fire Kills; the GOV.UK 2022 page says 8 times).
  - Consider CO alarms.
  - "Some children may not be woken by smoke alarms" (GOV.UK).
- NFCC: optical multi-sensor alarms, interlinked "where possible" (see gap-research.md §1).
- **Variation:** Fire Scotland and the Suffolk leaflet say test **weekly**; GOV.UK and Fire Kills say "at least monthly".

### Escape plan (Fire Kills *Escape*; GOV.UK)
- Best route: the normal way in and out. Have a second route. Keep exits, halls and windows clear. "Keep door and window keys in a safe but accessible place near the exit they fit. Never leave keys in locks where they could fall out." Agree a meeting point outside. Practise with everyone, including at night. Make it a game for children, and teach them "never to hide from fire or from firefighters". Review the plan if the layout changes. Plan for anyone who needs extra help.

### When the alarm sounds
- Fire Kills: get everyone out; don't collect belongings or investigate; close doors behind you; call 999 once outside ("never assume someone else has called"). In smoke, "crawl low under the smoke"; "cover your nose and mouth with a cloth if possible"; "feel doors with the back of your hand before opening – if they're warm, don't open them".
- Suffolk FRS, *What to do if there's a fire in your home* – https://www.suffolk.gov.uk/suffolk-fire-and-rescue-service/fire-safety-in-the-home/in-the-event-of-fire-in-your-house : "try not to panic"; "tell everyone in the house"; "stay low or crawl"; "do not stop to look for pets"; close the door of the fire room and all doors behind you; "touch it with the back of your hand; do not open it if it feels warm". For 999: give your full address (or what3words), the type of building (for example a two-storey house), and who is still inside and in which room.
- Clothes on fire (GOV.UK): "Stop, Drop and Roll"; smother the flames with a coat or blanket.

### If trapped (GOV.UK; Fire Kills)
- Get everyone into one room, ideally with a window and a phone. Close the door. Put bedding around the bottom of the door. "Call 999 then open the window and shout 'HELP FIRE'."
- Ground or first floor: "you may be able to escape through a window. Use bedding to cushion your fall and lower yourself down carefully. Don't jump." If the window won't open, "break the glass in the bottom corner. Make jagged edges safe with a towel or blanket." Higher up: "don't jump – wait for rescue".
- Some services say first floor only as a last resort (Warwickshire); see gap-research.md §1.

### Never go back in; pets
- Fire Kills: "Never go back for possessions, pets or people. Tell the fire service immediately if anyone is missing." Stay at the meeting point; keep back from the building; get medical help for anyone affected by smoke.
- Suffolk: going back in would "prevent the fire service from doing what they need to do and put your own life at risk".
- PDSA (verified 16/11/2026, gap-research.md §1): never go back for a pet; tell firefighters; keep leads and carriers easy to reach.
- GOV.UK: keep pets away from lit candles. LFB: supervise children and pets around open fires.

## 3. Fire-safety items for other sections of the guide

- **Power cuts:** Fire Scotland *Power cuts* – https://www.firescotland.gov.uk/at-home/power-cuts/ ; Dorset & Wiltshire FRS – https://www.dwfire.org.uk/safety/storms-and-power-cuts/power-cuts/
  - Use torches or battery lights. "Don't use candles unless you have no choice" (D&W). Keep torches and batteries easy to find; wind-up torches are "safe, cheap and never run out of power".
  - If you must use candles: in holders on a heat-resistant surface, away from anything that could burn; "do not use candles as a light source when moving from room to room"; never leave one alone; put them all out before going out or to sleep.
  - Turn off electrical equipment, especially electric hobs, but leave a light or radio on so you know when power returns. Call 105. Charge phones and power banks before storms.
  - Smell of gas: 0800 111 999; turn off appliances; open doors and windows; no smoking or switches.
- **Camping stoves, BBQs, generators:** "Never use a camping stove, portable barbecue, lit charcoal or generator in an enclosed space … can produce poisonous carbon monoxide" (D&W). "Never use any cooking items designed for outdoor usage like barbecues" indoors; "never change gas cartridges indoors or near ignition sources" (Fire Scotland). LFB: "never, ever use BBQs indoors or on balconies to cook or keep warm, and don't use candles as an alternative heat source." Devon & Somerset reported a gas BBQ used indoors during Storm Eunice; a CO alarm alerted the household (news search summary only).
- **Heating in a power cut / cold weather** (Fire Scotland; LFB): portable heaters off when out, out of the room or in bed; CO detectors in rooms with gas or paraffin heaters; never use portable gas-cylinder heaters in high-rise flats; never move a lit bottled-gas or oil heater; use gas and paraffin lamps only with ventilation and away from fabrics.
- **Carbon monoxide** links to the heating and power-cut sections: Fire Kills prevention says to service boilers yearly (Gas Safe) and to fit a CO alarm near gas appliances.
- **Free help:** online Home Fire Safety Check (Fire Kills/NFCC). Suffolk home fire safety visits depend on eligibility (gap-research.md §1).
