# v2 research: calories, walking out on foot, dogs, grab-bag reasons

Research note, 23 Nov 2026. Page fetching was blocked in this session (nutrition.org.uk, thebmc.co.uk and getoutside.ordnancesurvey.co.uk did not resolve), so these findings come from search-result extracts of the sources listed. Before publishing, check the exact figures against the original pages.
Labels: **[Evidence]** = official or peer-reviewed figure. **[Rule of thumb]** = practical guidance with no firm evidence base. **[Calc]** = my own arithmetic from a cited figure.

## 1. Energy needs (adults in their 50s)

- **[Evidence]** The NHS rough guide is about 2,500 kcal/day for an average man and 2,000 kcal/day for an average woman. These are also the reference intakes used on food labels. NHS, "Understanding calories": https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/healthy-weight/managing-your-weight/understanding-calories/
- **[Evidence]** SACN (2011) estimated average requirements. The age bands are 45–54 and 55–64; there is no 50–64 band.
  - Men 45–54: 10.8 MJ = about 2,580 kcal. Women 45–54: 8.8 MJ = about 2,100 kcal.
  - Men 55–64: 10.8 MJ = about 2,580 kcal. Women 55–64: 8.7 MJ = about 2,080 kcal.
  - These are population figures, not individual targets. They are based on a BMI of 22.5 and typical (not sedentary) activity.
  - SACN, Dietary Reference Values for Energy (2011): https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/sacn-dietary-reference-values-for-energy (PDF: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7edb37ed915d74e33f2d8f/SACN_Dietary_Reference_Values_for_Energy.pdf)
  - Table as reproduced by the British Nutrition Foundation: https://www.nutrition.org.uk/nutritional-information/nutrient-requirements
- **[Evidence, weak]** Cold. UKHSA's rapid evidence summary (search to Aug 2023, 58 studies, all small) found most evidence points to a *modest* increase of about **50–200 kcal/day** in extreme cold. UKHSA, "Additional calories in extreme cold weather" (power-outage series): https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6932d60ccdec734f4dff4311/Additional-calories-extreme-cold-weather-rapid-evidence-summary.pdf ; listing: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/health-emergency-planning
- **[Evidence]** Voluntary activity generates more heat than shivering. The cold also increases appetite, and older studies based on reported food intake may underestimate needs. US National Academies, *Nutritional Needs in Cold and in High-Altitude Environments* (1996): https://www.nap.edu/read/5197/chapter/17
- **[Rule of thumb]** In an unheated home, the best ways to keep warm are warm food and drinks and moving about, plus an extra 100–200 kcal/day (for example, one extra snack). Staying warm with layers matters more than eating extra.
- **[Evidence]** Walking with a pack, from the 2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities: walking with a day pack on level ground in town is 3.5 METs; carrying a 5–14 lb load at a moderate pace is 4.0; backpacking is 7.0; hiking with a daypack is 7.8. https://pacompendium.com/walking/
- **[Calc]** At roughly 1 kcal per kg per hour per MET, a 75 kg man burns about **260–525 kcal/hour** walking with a pack, and a 65 kg woman about **230–455 kcal/hour**. The higher figures apply to hills and heavier loads.
- **[Rule of thumb]** A 5–6 hour walk-out adds about **1,200–2,500 kcal** to a normal day. Plan for about 3,000–3,500 kcal (men) or 2,500–3,000 kcal (women) on that day. Eat little and often, about 200–300 kcal every hour or so.

## 2. Walking out on foot: vital kit

- **[Evidence/official]** Mountain Rescue England and Wales (via OS) lists:
  - map and compass
  - mobile phone (and possibly a power pack)
  - torch plus a spare or head torch
  - whistle
  - a watch
  - a first aid kit with essential medication
  - waterproofs and footwear with treaded soles
  - spare warm clothing, hat and gloves (even in summer)
  - ample food and drink, including high-energy snacks
  - survival bag
  
  Sources: OS/MREW, "Advice from Mountain Rescue": https://osmaps.com/discover/guides/mountain-safety/ ; MREW safety advice PDF: https://new.cro.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/MREWSafetyAdvice.pdf ; Lake District NPA walkers' checklist: https://lakedistrict.gov.uk/explore/things-to-do/walking/walkers-checklist/
- **[Official]** Carry a paper map because it needs no battery. Apps may not work without signal, and they drain the battery. OS with Mountain Rescue, "Navigation tips and advice": https://www.ordnancesurvey.co.uk/blog/navigation-tips-and-advice-from-mountain-rescue . OS Explorer (1:25,000) shows field boundaries, rights of way and access land, so it is the right scale for leaving on foot.
- **[Official]** International distress signal: **six long whistle blasts (or torch flashes) in a minute, pause one minute, repeat**. The reply is three. Keep signalling until someone reaches you. BMC, "Mountain incidents: what to do?": https://thebmc.co.uk/mountain-incidents-what-to-do ; OS/MREW (above).
- **[Official]** In an emergency, dial 999 or 112 and ask for Police, then Mountain Rescue (source as above).
- **[Evidence]** A survival bag is preferred to a foil blanket. Foil reflects radiant heat, but it barely insulates, is hard to wrap with cold, wet hands, and lets you get damp underneath. In a UK manikin study, purpose-made casualty bags insulated better than foil or plastic bags. Sources: Extreme Physiology & Medicine 2017: https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/s13728-017-0055-7 ; Physiological Society review: https://www.physoc.org/?p=120517
- **[Rule of thumb]** Pack weight: no more than about **10% of body weight for a day walk** and **about 20% for a multi-day pack**. Research (O'Shea) shows the % rule is crude: fitness, age and experience matter more, and heavier people should aim lower. Sources: REI, "How much should your pack weigh?": https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/backpacking-weight.html ; Advnture, "the 20% myth": https://advnture.com/features/how-much-should-backpack-weigh ; Science Connected: https://magazine.scienceconnected.org/2021/04/hikers-best-backpack-weight/
- **[Calc]** For someone in their 50s walking out with dogs, that works out at about **7–10 kg** for a 70–80 kg adult (plus dog kit). Keep it light enough to walk all day.
- **[Rule of thumb]** Choose a rucksack, not a holdall or wheeled case:
  - it leaves both hands free for leads, balance and stiles
  - a padded **hip belt** moves most of the load onto the hips
  - a chest strap stabilises it
  - rucksacks are not waterproof, so line it with a rucksack liner or bin bag and put kit in dry bags (phone, documents and spare clothes especially)
  - heavy items go close to the back and the middle
  - this is general outdoor practice, with no single official source

## 3. Dogs on foot

- **[Official, UK]** Pet grab bag: a lead or carrier for each animal, any medication, food, vaccination certificates, and food and water for several days in an airtight container. Sources: Medway Council: https://www.medway.gov.uk/info/200219/community/1444/looking_after_your_pets_in_an_emergency ; Norfolk Prepared: https://norfolkprepared.gov.uk/article/67035/Prepare-your-pets-for-emergencies ; Telford "Be pet ready" (rest centres may need vaccination certificates): https://www.telford.gov.uk/health-protection/emergencies/are-you-ready/be-pet-ready
- **[Law]** In public, a dog must wear a collar and tag with the owner's name and address (Control of Dogs Order 1992). A phone number is advised. Many councils advise *not* putting the dog's name on the tag. Dogs must also be microchipped with up-to-date details; the chip does not replace the tag. Sources: GOV.UK: https://www.gov.uk/government/news/chip-your-dog-and-check-your-chip ; Basildon: https://basildon.gov.uk/article/4919/Dog-collar-tags-and-microchipping ; Stevenage: https://www.stevenage.gov.uk/environment/animal-care-and-control/microchipping-dogs
  - Carry the microchip number, the database name, vet details and a recent photo of each dog in a dry bag.
- **[Evidence, approximate]** Dog water: typically about **40–60 ml per kg per day**, more for dogs on dry food, in exercise or in heat. For example, a 20 kg dog needs about 1 litre/day before exercise. Source: NCBI/PMC: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12636039/ . Carry water and a collapsible bowl. Don't let dogs drink floodwater.
- **[Rule of thumb]** Other dog kit:
  - harness plus a short lead (better control and less neck strain on a long walk), and a spare slip lead
  - waste bags
  - each dog's normal food in measured daily bags
  - medicines
  - a towel
  - a dog coat for short-coated or older dogs in the cold
  - a reflective or LED collar light
  - a muzzle if the dog may be handled while hurt (practise wearing it first)
  - **dog first aid kit**: bandage, vet wrap, gauze, saline, tick remover, tweezers and paw balm, plus booties for rough, icy or salted ground
  - check pads for cuts and ice balls between the toes
  
  Sources: AAHA hiking safety: https://www.aaha.org/your-pet/pet-owner-education/ask-aaha/10-safety-tips-for-hiking-with-your-dog/ ; Advnture dog first aid: https://www.advnture.com/features/doggy-first-aid
- **[Rule of thumb]** Dogs carrying their own packs: a fit, fully grown adult dog can carry about **10–12% of its body weight**, pack included. Build up from an empty pack. Puppies under about 2 years, older dogs, dogs with joint problems, long-backed/short-legged breeds and flat-faced breeds should not carry loads. Ask your vet. Sources (US): AKC: https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/lifestyle/teach-dog-wear-backpack/ ; Backpacker: https://www.backpacker.com/skills/how-to-safely-fit-load-dog-hiking-backpack/
- **[Rule of thumb]** A sling or backpack carrier for a small dog only makes sense for a dog of a few kg, and it counts towards your own pack weight. Medium and large dogs must walk, so plan the route and pace around the slowest dog. No UK charity guidance was found on this.
- Note: I found no specific Dogs Trust, Blue Cross or RSPCA (England & Wales) page on walking out in an emergency. The UK sources above are council emergency-planning pages.

## 4. One-line reasons for grab-bag items

- **Whistle**: carries much further than shouting and takes little energy. Six blasts is the distress signal.
- **Paper map (OS Explorer 1:25,000)**: works with no signal or battery and shows paths, rights of way and field boundaries.
- **Compass**: keeps you on course in fog, dark or unfamiliar ground, and works with the map.
- **Head torch**: leaves your hands free, and lets you signal (six flashes).
- **Phone + power bank**: for 999/112 and GPS location. The power bank keeps it alive. Keep the phone in flight mode to save battery.
- **Water**: you dehydrate even in cool weather, and dogs need it too.
- **High-energy food**: walking with a pack burns about 250–500 kcal an hour, and food helps keep you warm.
- **Waterproof jacket and trousers**: wet clothing loses heat fast, and staying dry is key to avoiding hypothermia.
- **Hat, gloves, spare warm layer**: you lose heat quickly once you stop moving.
- **Survival bag (or foil blanket)**: cuts wind, wet and heat loss if you must stop. A bag beats a foil blanket.
- **First aid kit + medicines**: deals with blisters, cuts and sprains, and keeps you on essential medication.
- **Watch**: for timing whistle signals and pace, without draining the phone.
- **Cash, ID, keys, copies of documents (in dry bag)**: card machines and phones may be down, and you'll need to prove who you are.
- **Rucksack with hip belt + liner/dry bags**: hands free for leads and balance, weight on the hips, and kit stays dry.
- **Dog lead/harness + ID**: keeps dogs under control, and gets them returned if separated.
- **Collapsible bowl, dog food, waste bags, dog first aid**: covers the dogs' basic needs on the move.
- **Pencil and paper**: for leaving notes and recording details.
